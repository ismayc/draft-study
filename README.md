# Draft model: does college production beat the scouts?

**Question.** The draft pick *is* the scouting industry's consensus
forecast. Given a player's final college season, does box-score production
predict NBA outcomes beyond what the pick already encodes?

<!-- terms -->
> **Terms used in this analysis.** Dotted-underlined terms anywhere below repeat these definitions on hover ([full glossary](../docs/glossary.md)).
>
> - **IRLS** — Iteratively reweighted least squares - the standard algorithm for fitting a logistic regression.
> - **AUC** — Area under the ROC curve: the probability the model ranks a random positive case above a random negative one. 0.5 is a coin flip.

## Data

| Source | What | How |
|---|---|---|
| [barttorvik.com](https://barttorvik.com) advanced stats (the cbbdata/toRvik upstream) | 56,781 college player-seasons, 2011-2022, 67 columns | free CSV endpoint, no key; header pinned empirically and gated |
| nba_api `DraftHistory` | draft year, pick, NBA `PERSON_ID` | one call |
| nba_api `LeagueDashPlayerStats` | NBA minutes, 2011-12 .. 2024-25 (the outcome) | 14 cached calls |

NBA minutes join by **ID**, not name; the one name join in the study
(Torvik ↔ DraftHistory) is gated at 98%+ with every historical mismatch
audited into an explicit nickname/name-change alias table (Bam Adebayo,
Mo Bamba, Bones Hyland, ...).

## Design

- Sample: drafted players whose final college season is 2011-2022 (n=567).
- Outcome: ≥2,000 NBA minutes within three seasons of the draft.
- Models: logistic `M0: log(pick)` vs `M1: log(pick) + 15 college features`
  (age, height, OBPM/DBPM, usage, TS%, rebound/assist/turnover/stock rates,
  3PA volume, FT rate, class), fit by the same hand-rolled <abbr title="Iteratively reweighted least squares - the standard algorithm for fitting a logistic regression.">IRLS</abbr> as
  `../shot-quality-study`, mirrored in R.
- **Era-based holdout**: train on classes 2011-2018, evaluate on 2019-2022.
  Random splits leak era effects; time splits are how a front office would
  actually use the model.

## Findings

Every number below is generated by `python/04_findings.py` from `output/`,
not typed by hand. Both implementations (R and Python) reconcile to numeric
tolerance (observed ~1e-13) before this is written.

### The setup

567 drafted college players, classes 2011-2022, outcome =
2,000+ NBA minutes within three seasons of the draft (base rate
54%). Train: classes 2011-2018. Test: classes 2019-2022 — drafts the
model never saw.

### The headline is a negative result, reported as one

| model | train <abbr title="Area under the ROC curve: the probability the model ranks a random positive case above a random negative one. 0.5 is a coin flip.">AUC</abbr> | test <abbr title="Area under the ROC curve: the probability the model ranks a random positive case above a random negative one. 0.5 is a coin flip.">AUC</abbr> | train log-loss | test log-loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| pick only | 0.844 | 0.827 | 0.497 | 0.528 |
| pick + college stats | 0.861 | 0.827 | 0.470 | 0.538 |

In sample, college production looks useful (+0.017
<abbr title="Area under the ROC curve: the probability the model ranks a random positive case above a random negative one. 0.5 is a coin flip.">AUC</abbr>). Out of sample the advantage is +0.000 <abbr title="Area under the ROC curve: the probability the model ranks a random positive case above a random negative one. 0.5 is a coin flip.">AUC</abbr>
and log-loss actually degrades — the textbook signature of overfitting, shown
on real data. The draft pick is the scouting industry's aggregate forecast,
and **on this outcome, at this sample size, box-score college production adds
nothing the scouts had not already priced in.** That is the finding. A
junior analyst's version of this study ends with the train-set table and a
claim that college stats "improve the model by two <abbr title="Area under the ROC curve: the probability the model ranks a random positive case above a random negative one. 0.5 is a coin flip.">AUC</abbr> points"; the era-based
holdout is the difference between that story and the truth.

What it does NOT say: that college stats are useless (richer outcomes,
bigger samples, or interactions may yet pay), or that scouting is
unbeatable — public models like DARKO-derived draft work use far richer
inputs. It says the cheap version of the claim fails honestly.

### What a draft slot is actually worth

The pick-only curve is itself decision-relevant (see
`figures/fig1_pick_curve_py.html`): P(rotation player) falls from
99.9% at pick 1 (an extrapolation to the curve's edge — read it as
"nearly certain") to a coin flip around pick 28, and keeps falling —
draft capital is front-loaded exactly the way trade valuations assume.

### Where the model out-called the scouts (test classes)

Largest positive residuals — players the model's inputs undervalued least
badly versus where they went: Jabari Walker (pick 57, 3,069 min), Jalen McDaniels (pick 52, 2,092 min), Daniel Gafford (pick 38, 2,844 min), Isaiah Joe (pick 49, 2,388 min). Individual residuals are anecdotes,
not evidence; they are listed because a front office will always ask.

### Honest limitations

- Binary outcome and a blunt one; minutes confound opportunity with merit.
- College-only: international and G-League-Ignite paths are out of frame,
  and two lottery picks (Sharpe, Kanter) never logged a college minute.
- 567 players is small; the negative result is about THIS feature set at
  THIS sample size, not a theorem about college data.
- Name matching is gated (98.8% draft-year agreement; every mismatch was
  audited by hand into an explicit alias table in the code).
